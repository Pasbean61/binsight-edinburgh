The problem

Fixed timetables meet waste that never arrives on schedule

Edinburgh is a city of tenements and flats. Dozens of households share each on-street communal bin, and how much goes in changes every day. A timetable can’t see short-term lets, students moving out, the festivals or a busy Saturday, so it forces a bad choice: collect too often and waste money, or too rarely and live with overflow.

The maths

Five steps behind one plan

Each step of BinSight is a piece of mathematics we wrote ourselves: a simulator of how bins fill, calibrated to the council’s own complaint rate; a random-forest forecast; a fullest-first decision rule; one routing model solved by simulated annealing; and a before-and-after test. Every widget below runs on tomorrow’s real stops and today’s model output.

How communal bins fill: derived from how the council runs the service

The council empties communal general-waste bins every K = 2 days Real, so a stop holds about two days of its households’ waste. If a stop is on average a share U full when the truck comes, it fills

ρi = U / K · eσZi,   Zi ~ N(0, 1),   σ = 0.35

of its capacity a day; σ spreads the stops, because some serve more flats than others. Households drop one 30 L kitchen bag at a time, so in hour t

Nit ~ Poisson(λit),   λit = (capi ρi / 30 L) · a(hour) · wt / 24
  • a: the hour-of-day profile, mean 1, quiet at night with morning and evening peaks (an assumption).
  • wt ∝ e−0.15·1[rain ≥ 0.5 mm] (1 + 0.01 (T − 10 °C)) (1 + 0.10·1[holiday]), rescaled to mean 1, from real hourly Edinburgh weather and real public and school holidays. The three effect sizes are assumptions.
  • Calibration. Of about 100,000 communal-bin collections a month, 1–1.5 % draw a complaint (council report, 2017). We run the every-two-days timetable for a grid of U and keep the one at which 1 % of collections overflow: U = 0.415. Bins are then on average about 44 % full when the timetable empties them.
  • Sensors. A reading is the true fill plus N(0, 1²) percentage points.

The fill process is simulated because the council does not publish sensor data; the stops, bins, weather and holidays are real.

A random-forest forecast of how fast each stop fills

Every morning at 05:00 one random forest (100 trees, scikit-learn defaults) predicts, for every stop, how many percentage points it will gain per hour over the next day, ĝi. The projected fill at the next chance of a truck (14:00 tomorrow) is

ŷi = yi + ĝi · hours

where yi is the latest reading. ŷi may pass 100 %: a stop projected at 130 % is expected to overflow well before the truck could come.

  • Inputs, only what a real system has: latest reading; fill rate since last emptied; change over the last 24 h; hours since last emptied; capacity; weekday; hours of rain and mean temperature before the next chance of a truck; share of those hours on a holiday.
  • Target: the reading 24 h later minus the reading now, over 24, on every past morning when no truck came in between. The forest is retrained every morning on all the days seen so far: – rows for tomorrow’s forecast.
  • Why regression, not a classifier of “will it overflow?”. The fleet cannot reach every stop at risk, so the order among them decides who waits. A classifier gave about 1,550 stops a probability of at least 0.99, so the order at the top was arbitrary. The projected fill still separates a stop that fills in two hours from one that fills in thirty.

Who gets a truck: fullest first until the fleet is full

One rule, on the projected fill ŷi from step 2:

sort the stops by ŷi, fullest first, and take them while the fleet has time; never take a stop with ŷi < 80 %
  • The floor. 80 % is the threshold smart-bin systems commonly use to flag a bin for collection. Below it a stop can safely wait, even on a quiet day.
  • The time a stop uses. ti = si + θ · (drive from its nearest other candidate), with lifting time si = 2 + 1 · binsi minutes and rain slowdown θ. Each full 60 m³ load adds a 25-minute tip. The stops are taken while
    Σtaken ti + 25 · ⌈Σtaken qi / Q⌉ ≤ 0.95 · K · 480 min
  • Rough on purpose. Routing (step 4) has the final say: if the trips do not fit the fleet, it leaves out the least full stops first.

Routing: one model, one algorithm

On the road graph (– km of Edinburgh streets from OpenStreetMap, shortest paths by Dijkstra), truck k makes trips r; xijkr = 1 if it drives i → j, zikr = 1 if it empties stop i, ukr = 1 if the trip is made. Cost cij = £1.45·km + £75·θ·hours; leaving stop i out costs its prize πi = £60 × ŷi/100, its share of an overflow.

min   Σ cij xijkr + Σi πi(1 − Σkr zikr)
s.t.   Σkr zikr ≤ 1   (each stop at most once)
   Σi qi zikr ≤ Q ukr,   Q = 60 m³ per trip, then tip at the depot
   Σr (θ·drive + lift + 25 min tip) ≤ 480 min per truck, 24 trucks
   flow in = flow out, no subtours,   x, z, u ∈ {0, 1}

Weather and holidays sit in the constraints. The load qi(w) = capi · min(1, (yi + bi Σh wh) / 100) grows with the hourly factor wh = e−0.15·rain · (1 + 0.01(T − 10)) · (1 + 0.10·holiday) until the truck arrives. Rain also slows driving: θ = 1 + 0.10 × the share of the shift’s hours with rain. All four effect sizes are assumptions.

This is NP-hard, and tomorrow has – stops, so we solve it with simulated annealing: start by filling truck after truck with the nearest stop that still fits; then, again and again, change the plan a little (move a stop, swap two, reverse part of a trip, swap the ends of two trips, drop or add a stop) and keep the change if it lowers the objective, or with probability e−Δ/T if it raises it, cooling T geometrically. A change that breaks a constraint is never made.

Try it on 12 real stops

Held–Karp dynamic programming, C(S, j) = mini∈S C(S ∖ {j}, i) + cij, solves the tour exactly over 212 × 12 states, in your browser. Lines are straight; costs are road costs.

How do we know it works? Before and after

Three policies run on the same simulated city, with the same deposits, weather and sensor noise (common random numbers):

  • Timetable: every stop every second day, in fixed geographic rounds. Today’s practice.
  • Reactive: every stop reading ≥ 80 % at 05:00. Sensors, no forecast.
  • BinSight: forecast → fullest first → routes.

Same fleet for all three. No public figure says how many of Edinburgh’s refuse trucks serve communal general waste, so the timetable is routed without a fleet limit and the trucks it needs on its busiest round, –, become the fleet for every policy. BinSight is compared with today’s practice at equal resources.

What the test cannot show
  • Fill is simulated; the forecast is trained and tested on the same simulator.
  • Two structural assumptions (spread of fill speed, hour-of-day profile) and the size of the weather and holiday effects have no direct source.
  • The complaint rate used for calibration is a lower bound on overflows.
  • One-way streets and turn restrictions are ignored; tomorrow’s weather is assumed known; trucks tip at Seafield, while real residual waste goes to Millerhill.
  • Simulated annealing gives a good plan, not a proven optimum.

Business model

Three partners, one service

We sell sensors that fit onto existing bins and a platform that turns their data into better collection decisions. The council pays for the sensors and platform, residents add reports, and recycling facilities buy the forecasts. Each partner makes the service more valuable to the others.
