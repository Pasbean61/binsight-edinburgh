The problem

Bins fill at different speeds. A timetable can’t see that.

Edinburgh is a city of tenements. Instead of a wheelie bin for every home, 10,657 communal bins stand on its streets, each shared by the flats around it. Every bin fills at its own speed, yet the trucks still follow a fixed timetable. So some bins overflow before the truck arrives, while others are emptied half full. In one summer quarter, people reported 6,006 full or overflowing bins. And money is tight: collection now costs £1.35 million more a year, and the council must find £20.2 million of savings this year. A better timetable won’t fix this. The city needs to see inside its bins.

Same fortnight, two ways to collect

Day 0

The timetable empties every bin on days 3, 6, 9 and 12, whether it is full or not.

Rule used by BinSight here: empty a bin today if tomorrow’s forecast is 80 % or more. The real engine applies the same 80 % floor to Edinburgh’s 4,532 stops, below, taking the fullest first while the fleet has time.

Our answer

Measure, forecast, route, collect

BinSight gives the city that view. It works in four steps, and it starts with a sensor that costs two pounds.

Step 1

Measure

A small sensor under the lid measures how full the bin is, every hour.

Step 2

Forecast

A model combines those readings with the weather, the day of the week and holidays, and predicts how full each bin will be tomorrow.

Step 3

Route

Bins forecast to reach 80 % go on tomorrow’s list, fullest first, and the trucks get the shortest routes to them.

Step 4

Collect

Crews follow the plan, and residents can report a problem in an app.

How the plan is made

Five steps behind the plan

How does a bin fill up?

The council does not share its sensor data, so we build our own. People drop bags at random times: more in the evening, fewer in the rain.

\[ \underbrace{N_{it}}_{\mathclap{\substack{\text{bags in bin } i \\ \text{in hour } t}}} \quad\sim\quad \operatorname{Poisson}\bigl(\,\underbrace{\lambda_{it}}_{\mathclap{\substack{\text{average} \\ \text{rate}}}}\,\bigr) \] \[ \lambda_{it} \;\;\propto\;\; \underbrace{\rho_i}_{\mathclap{\substack{\text{how busy} \\ \text{the stop is}}}} \quad\times\quad \underbrace{a_h}_{\mathclap{\substack{\text{time} \\ \text{of day}}}} \quad\times\quad \underbrace{w_t}_{\mathclap{\substack{\text{weather,} \\ \text{holidays}}}} \]
  • Random, like real life. Two weeks with the same average can look very different. That is why bins overflow by surprise.
  • Matched to reality. We tune one number so that the simulated timetable overflows on @target@ % of collections, the same as the council’s complaint rate.
  • Real inputs. The bins, the weather and the holidays are real. Only the bags are simulated.

Every parameter and its source is in model/README.md.

How full will it be tomorrow?

Every morning a machine-learning model (a random forest) learns how fast each bin is filling. Then we extend the line.

\[ \underbrace{\hat y_i}_{\text{forecast}} \;=\; \underbrace{y_i}_{\substack{\text{fill} \\ \text{now}}} \;+\; \underbrace{H}_{\substack{\text{hours} \\ \text{ahead}}} \times \underbrace{\hat g_i}_{\substack{\text{filling} \\ \text{speed}}} \]
\[ \text{Example:}\quad @exY@\,\% + @H@\ \text{h} \times @exG@\ \text{points/h} \approx @exP@\,\% \]
  • What the model looks at: the latest reading, how fast the bin has been filling, the weekday, and the weather and holidays ahead.
  • Hours ahead: until 14:00 tomorrow, the bin’s next chance of a truck.
  • Over 100 % is allowed. A forecast of 130 % means the bin will overflow well before the truck comes, so it goes to the top of the list.

Which bins get a truck?

Sort the bins from fullest to emptiest. Take them from the top until the trucks have no time left.

\[ \underbrace{\hat y_{(1)} \ge \hat y_{(2)} \ge \cdots \ge @floor@\,\%}_{\text{fullest first, skip below @floor@ \%}} \] \[ \underbrace{\tau_{(1)} + \tau_{(2)} + \cdots + \tau_{(k)}}_{\text{time the chosen stops need}} \;\le\; \underbrace{m \times T}_{\text{trucks} \,\times\, \text{shift}} \]
  • Fullest first. The bins closest to overflowing are always served first.
  • Skip below @floor@ %. A bin forecast under @floor@ % can safely wait, so no truck is sent.
  • Time per stop \(\tau\): lifting the bins, driving there, and tipping when the truck is full.

Routing: one model, solved by simulated annealing

Step 3 chose tomorrow’s stops. This step decides which truck empties each one, in what order, and when it drives back to Seafield to tip.

The model. On the road graph (– km of Edinburgh streets from OpenStreetMap, shortest paths by Dijkstra), truck k makes trips r. Driving i → j costs cij = £1.45·km + £75·θ·hours. Leaving stop i out costs its prize πi = £60 × ŷi/100, its share of an overflow.

min   Σ cij xijkr + Σi πi(1 − Σkr zikr)
Σkr zikr ≤ 1   each stop at most once
Σi qi zikr ≤ Q = 60 m³   one load per trip
Σr (θ·drive + lift + tip) ≤ 480 min   each of – trucks
flow in = flow out, no subtours,  x, z, u ∈ {0, 1}

The algorithm: simulated annealing. The model is NP-hard and tomorrow has – stops, so no exact solver finishes in minutes. Annealing searches instead:

  1. Start. Fill truck after truck, trip after trip, with the nearest stop that still fits.
  2. Propose a small change. Pick a stop v and one of its 20 nearest stops w, then: move v next to w · swap them · reverse the path between them (2-opt) · cross two trips at them (2-opt*) · drop v · give v a new trip. Changes that break a constraint are never made.
  3. Accept or reject. Δ = new cost − old cost. Keep the change if Δ ≤ 0; if it is worse, keep it anyway with probability e−Δ/T.
  4. Cool. The temperature T falls geometrically over the time budget. Early on, worse plans are often kept, so the search can climb out of a dead end. At the end it only goes downhill.
  5. Return the best plan seen.

Weather and holidays sit in the constraints. The load qi grows with the hourly factor wh (rain, temperature, holidays) until the truck arrives, and rain slows driving: θ = 1 + 0.10 × the share of the shift’s hours with rain. Both effect sizes are assumptions.
