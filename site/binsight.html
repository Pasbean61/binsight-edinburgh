The problem

Fixed timetables meet waste that never arrives on schedule

Edinburgh is a city of tenements and flats. Dozens of households share each on-street communal bin, and how much goes in changes every day. A timetable can’t see short-term lets, students moving out, the festivals or a busy Saturday, so it forces a bad choice: collect too often and waste money, or too rarely and live with overflow.

The maths

Five models behind one plan

Each step of BinSight is a piece of mathematics we wrote and tested ourselves: a stochastic model of how bins fill, a statistical forecast, a decision rule, a routing algorithm, and checks against exact answers. Every widget below runs on tomorrow’s real stops and today’s model output.

How a bin fills: a compound Poisson process

Households drop bags at random moments. In hour t, stop i receives N bags, and each bag has a random volume V. The litres added are

Xit = V1 + … + VN,   N ~ Poisson(λit),   V ~ Gamma(k = 2, mean 35 L)
λit = (Di / v̄) · a(hour, weekday) · wt · cit / 24
  • Di = capi · ρi: litres a day. Each stop’s fill share ρi is log-normal, median 0.20 a day.
  • a: the hour-of-week profile, mean 1, with morning and evening peaks and busier weekends.
  • wt = e−0.15·1[rain ≥ 0.5 mm] (1 + 0.01 (T − 10 °C)), from real hourly Edinburgh weather.
  • cit = 1.25 on Friday to Sunday in the City Centre ward, for visitors and short lets.

Because a sum of n Gamma(k, θ) volumes is Gamma(nk, θ), a whole hour for all 4,533 stops is one exact vectorised draw. The mean and variance follow from the law of total variance:

E[X] = λ v̄,   Var[X] = λ E[V2] = λ v̄2(1 + 1/k)  ⇒  φ = Var/E = v̄(1 + 1/k) = 52.5 L

The forecaster never sees these settings. It estimates φ = – L from sensor readings alone, close to the true 52.5 L. The fill process is simulated because the council does not publish sensor data; the weather, places and bins are real.

A forecast with honest uncertainty

The forecast learns only from sensor readings, in three steps.

  1. City-wide shape. Least squares on the log of the mean hourly increase across all stops:
    log gt = β0 + h(hour) + d(weekday) + βrain 1[rain] + βtemp(T − 10)
    This gives mt, the share of a day’s waste expected in hour t. Fitted rain effect: –.
  2. Each stop’s rate. Each day gives one observation o of litres per average day, and
    ri ← (1 − β) ri + β o,   β = 0.3
    Exponential smoothing tracks each stop and forgets old days geometrically.
  3. Variance from three sources. With μL = ri Σ mt the litres expected before the next truck:
    σi2 = (100/capi)2 ( φ μL + β/(2 − β) ei2 μL2 ) + σs2
    These are Poisson arrival noise, the error of the smoothed rate (the variance of an EWMA), and sensor noise.

Fill only rises between collections, so the risk of overflowing before the next truck (by 14:00 the day after) is

pi = 1 − Φ( (100 − μi) / σi )

Who gets a truck: a chance-constrained cost rule

Skipping stop i tomorrow risks an overflow with probability pi, at cost co = £60 (complaint, side waste, street cleaning). Collecting it costs the extra driving and lifting it adds to the routes, Δi.

Going early is cheaper than it looks. A stop emptied today will not need emptying later. Over a renewal cycle of τi days from empty to full, emptying it ki days before it would otherwise have to go wastes ki/τi of a cycle. So the net value of collecting it tomorrow is

vi = pi co − Δi ki / τi,   ki = 1 + max(0, (100 − μi) / gi)

where gi = 100/τi is the fill rate in % a day. The fleet has 24 × 480 truck-minutes, and stop i uses ti of them, so tomorrow is a knapsack problem. Relaxing the time limit with a Lagrange multiplier λ (a price per truck-minute) gives the rule

collect i  ⇔  pi > α   or   vi > λ ti
  • α = 0.20 is a service standard: no stop is skipped with more than a 20 % chance of overflowing (a chance constraint).
  • λ = 0 when the trucks have room; otherwise bisection finds the smallest λ whose choice fits the shift. Tomorrow: –.
  • Δi and ti depend on which other stops are chosen. We start from the cheapest insertion between nearby urgent stops, route, then use the exact insertion cost into the routed trips and the exact removal saving, correct the time estimate by what routing really used, and choose again.
  • Costs: £1.45 per km (diesel and wear) and £75 per truck-hour (driver, two loaders, vehicle).

With λ = 0, as here, a stop goes if it is right of the dashed line or above the green one (p·co > Δk/τ). The router then trims to the fleet.

Routing: a multi-trip vehicle-routing problem

On the road graph (– km of Edinburgh streets from OpenStreetMap, shortest paths by Dijkstra), with cost cij = £1.45·km + £75·hours:

min   Σk Σi≠j cij xijk
s.t.   every chosen stop is visited exactly once
   Σi ∈ trip qi ≤ Q = 60 m³ per load, then tip at the depot
   Σ (drive + lift + tip) ≤ 480 min per truck, 24 trucks
   uj ≥ ui + qj − Q(1 − xij)   (no subtours),   x ∈ {0, 1}

This is NP-hard, and tomorrow has – stops. We solve it with our own heuristic in four stages:

  1. Clarke–Wright savings: sij = c0i + c0j − cij, merging trips in order of saving while load and time allow.
  2. Local search: 2-opt and Or-opt inside trips; relocate and swap between trips, over each stop’s 20 nearest neighbours.
  3. Simulated annealing: accept a worse move with probability e−Δ/T, cooling T geometrically, then local search again.
  4. Pack trips into shifts by first-fit decreasing; if the fleet is short, drop the stops of least net value.

Try it on 12 real stops

Held–Karp dynamic programming, C(S, j) = mini∈S C(S ∖ {j}, i) + cij, solves the tour exactly over 212 × 12 states, in your browser. Lines are straight; costs are road costs.

How do we know it works?

Against exact optima. On 30 small real instances (12 stops in one ward, loads from 50 to 100 % full, capacity forcing several trips), the MILP above is solved to proven optimality with HiGHS and compared with each stage of the heuristic.

Against a lower bound. No plan can cost less than max(Held–Karp 1-tree bound, 2 Σ qi c0i / Q). The 1-tree bound is improved by subgradient optimisation; the radial bound counts the trips each litre forces to and from the depot.

Business model

Three partners, one service

We sell sensors that fit onto existing bins and a platform that turns their data into better collection decisions. The council pays for the sensors and platform, residents add reports, and recycling facilities buy the forecasts. Each partner makes the service more valuable to the others.
